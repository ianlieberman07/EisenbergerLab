Helping others feels good—but our work suggests that feeling may be doing more for us than we realize. We have shown that prosocial acts engage neural regions involved in mammalian caregiving and reward, regions that can in turn dampen threat-related responding in the amygdala and downstream physiological stress responses. This raises a central question: can giving to others actually improve the health of the giver? Across studies, we have found that providing support to a loved one before a stressor reduces blood pressure and sympathetic nervous system reactivity; that people randomly assigned to give to others (rather than themselves) after stress recover more quickly; and that increases in support-giving are associated with reduced amygdala reactivity to threat, and reductions in inflammation. Extending this work to older adults, we have found that a generativity intervention—cultivating the desire to help others, especially younger generations—improves mental health, increases social engagement, and reduces proinflammatory gene expression. We have also found that prosocial acts reduce loneliness and improve mental health, in both undergraduate and large community samples. We are currently very interested in the mechanisms that distinguish prosocial rewards from other types of rewarding experiences, leading to questions like: Why don’t we crave prosocial reward in the same way that we crave other rewards? And why do prosocial rewards sometimes feel more “fulfilling?”
