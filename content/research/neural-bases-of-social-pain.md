When people feel rejected or left out, they often reach for physical pain words—"*hurt* feelings," "*broken* hearts," feeling "*crushed*." Is this just a figure of speech, or does social pain really borrow the machinery of physical pain? Because social connection has been essential to mammalian survival, we have argued that socially painful experiences may recruit some of the same neural circuitry that processes physical pain, using the pain signal to keep us from becoming disconnected. Using behavioral, neuroimaging, pharmacological, and genetic methods, we have shown that social rejection activates pain-related neural regions such as the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex and anterior insula; that individuals more sensitive to one kind of pain are also more sensitive to the other; and that manipulating one kind of pain—through social support, inflammation, or even over-the-counter analgesics—shifts the other in parallel. We have also traced how activity in these pain- and threat-related regions helps translate negative social experiences into downstream physiological stress responses, including cortisol and inflammatory reactivity, across healthy samples, people at risk for depression, and breast cancer survivors.
