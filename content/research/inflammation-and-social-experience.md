If negative social experiences can alter our physiology, does the reverse hold—can the body's immune activity reshape our social world? Research on inflammation has focused largely on depression and negative mood, leaving its specifically *social* consequences relatively unexplored. Our work has shown that heightened inflammation increases feelings of social disconnection and enhances sensitivity to negative social experiences, including rejection, negative feedback, and threatening social images. At the same time, while inflammation dampens neural reward responses to non-social rewards like money, it can *heighten* reward-related responses to positive social stimuli—images of close others, or receiving positive feedback. We have proposed that the sick state induces a heightened sense of social vulnerability, which leads to greater vigilance toward those who might pose a threat, alongside greater pull toward those who might provide care. This reframes the dominant "inflammation causes social withdrawal" model in favor of a more nuanced pattern of social sensitivity. We are now testing the flip side of this account by asking whether reducing inflammation pharmacologically can relieve loneliness and depression.
