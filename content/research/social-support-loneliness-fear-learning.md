Loved ones can reduce our pain and calm our stress responses—a phenomenon known as "social buffering"—but *how* they do this has remained unclear. We have shown that simply viewing a photograph of a loved one during painful stimulation not only reduced pain distress but also engaged ventromedial prefrontal cortex, a region central to extinguishing fear and dampening threat responses. Building on this, we have been testing whether social support figures act as *prepared fear suppressors*: mirror images of the "prepared fear stimuli" (snakes, spiders) that are easy to learn to fear and hard to extinguish. Because close others have historically promoted survival, they may be uniquely resistant to becoming fear cues—and may help extinguish other fears as well. We have shown that people are essentially unable to condition a fear response to their social support figures, that support figures attenuate fear acquisition to novel cues, that they facilitate the extinction of already-conditioned fears, and that physically warm stimuli behave similarly. More recently, we have turned to the opposite case: loneliness. Consistent with animal work showing that social isolation augments fear learning and delays extinction, we find that lonelier individuals show impaired fear extinction—and that reminders of a social support figure can reduce that impairment. Ongoing work examines the neural underpinnings of these effects and whether support reminders or warmth can reverse loneliness-related deficits in fear learning.
